# Penalty, Power, Presence? Rethinking the Three Tenses of Salvation

## Summary

Many churches teach salvation in three tenses:

- **Justification:** saved from the *penalty* of sin (past)
- **Sanctification:** being saved from the *power* of sin (present)
- **Glorification:** will be saved from the *presence* of sin (future)

A related phrase says: *"We are saved by works — not ours, but His."*

These formulas are standard teaching in much of evangelical and Reformed Protestantism, and their defenders see them as faithful summaries of Scripture. This study presents the case that relational-gospel and Jewish-lens readers make against them, where the formulas came from, and what those readers offer instead. The traditional response comes at the end.

---

## 1. Why Critics Say the Formula Doesn't Match Scripture

### 1.1 The Bible doesn't use the words on this timeline

The scheme puts justification in the past, sanctification in the present and glorification in the future. The New Testament mixes the tenses freely.

| Term | Tense used in Scripture | References |
|---|---|---|
| Sanctification | **Past**: "you were sanctified" | 1 Cor 6:11; 1 Cor 1:2; Heb 10:10 |
| Glorification | **Past**: "those whom he justified he also glorified" | Rom 8:30 |
| Salvation | **Past** | Eph 2:8 |
| Salvation | **Present** | 1 Cor 1:18 |
| Salvation | **Future** | Rom 5:9 |

In 1 Cor 6:11, sanctification is even listed *before* justification.

In Hebrew thought, **kadosh** (holy) means first of all *set apart to God*. It describes a relationship and a belonging, not only a gradual moral improvement.

### 1.2 "Penalty, power, presence" frames sin as an object, not a broken relationship

The Hebrew Bible uses several words for sin:

- **chet**: missing the mark
- **avon**: twistedness, iniquity
- **pesha**: rebellion, breach of covenant

Each describes a rupture in relationship with God and neighbor. A courtroom penalty to be cancelled is only part of the picture. The biblical remedy is **teshuvah**: returning, reconciliation, restored fellowship.

### 1.3 "The presence of sin" implies escape, not new creation

The biblical hope is **resurrection and the renewal of creation** (Isa 65:17–25; Rom 8:19–23; Rev 21:1–5). It is not removal to a sin-free elsewhere. Critics hear a Greek, Platonic undertone in the idea of being taken out of the world.

### 1.4 "Saved by works — not ours, but His" can sideline faithful obedience

The phrase usually expresses the Reformed idea that Christ's perfect law-keeping is credited to the believer (*active obedience imputed*). Critics raise four points:

- Scripture repeatedly says people are judged **according to their deeds** (Rom 2:6–7; Matt 25:31–46; 2 Cor 5:10; Rev 20:12).
- **Faith without works is dead** (Jas 2:14–26).
- Scripture never explicitly says Christ's law-obedience is "imputed." The doctrine is inferred from passages such as Rom 5:19 and Phil 3:9, and some Protestant scholars dispute it.
- **Pistis / emunah** (faith) means trust *and* faithfulness, an embodied loyalty rather than only mental assent.

## 2. Where These Formulations Came From

| Era | Source | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 4th–5th c. | **Augustine** | Original guilt, grace and merit framed in Latin legal categories. The Eastern church leaned toward salvation as healing and union (*theosis*). |
| 1098 | **Anselm, *Cur Deus Homo*** | Satisfaction theory: the atonement repays God's offended honor, drawing on feudal and legal models. |
| 16th c. | **Luther and Calvin** | Forensic justification and "alien righteousness," in reaction to medieval merit systems. First-century Judaism was read as if it were the same kind of works-righteousness religion. |
| 17th c. | **Protestant scholasticism and the Westminster Confession** | Systematized the *ordo salutis* (order of salvation), the covenant of works and active-obedience imputation. |
| 19th–20th c. | **Keswick holiness, dispensationalism, popular preaching** | Popularized the "penalty / power / presence" triad as a teaching aid. |

**The common thread critics point to** is a Western legal and individualistic lens laid over Hebrew texts that are covenantal and communal.

## 3. The Jewish Lens

### 3.1 Grace comes before law

God rescues Israel *first* ("I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of Egypt," Ex 20:2) and gives Torah *afterward* as the way of life for a redeemed people. E.P. Sanders called this **covenantal nomism**: you get in by grace and stay in by faithful response. Torah-keeping was never meant to earn salvation.

### 3.2 "Works of the law" as identity markers

Scholars of the New Perspective on Paul (James Dunn, N.T. Wright) read "works of the law" mainly as markers of Jewish identity: circumcision, Sabbath and food laws. Paul's argument is about whether **Gentiles must become Jews** to belong to God's family. It is not mainly about moral effort versus passive faith.

### 3.3 The faithfulness *of* the Messiah

Many scholars translate **pistis Christou** (Gal 2:16; Rom 3:22) as *"the faithfulness of Christ"* rather than *"faith in Christ."* On this reading we are saved by His work, meaning his faithful life, death and resurrection as Israel's representative. We share in it through **union and loyal trust**, not a ledger transfer.

### 3.4 Righteousness (tzedakah) is covenant faithfulness

God's righteousness is his **saving faithfulness to his promises** (Ps 98:2–3; Isa 46:13). Human righteousness is right relationship lived out:

> "Do justice, love kindness, and walk humbly with your God." (Mic 6:8)

## 4. The Relational Gospel

### 4.1 Salvation (yeshuah) is deliverance into relationship

- **Knowing God:** "This is eternal life, that they know you" (John 17:3)
- **Reconciliation:** 2 Cor 5:18–20
- **Adoption:** Rom 8:15; Gal 4:4–7
- **A new heart:** Torah written on the heart by the Spirit (Jer 31:33; Ezek 36:26–27)

The past, present and future tenses are **one relationship growing toward fullness**. They are not three separate legal transactions.

### 4.2 Holiness is imitating God

"Be holy, for I am holy" (Lev 19:2) is followed by practical commands about neighbors, the poor and strangers. Holiness means reflecting God's character in community.

### 4.3 The goal is God's presence

The end of the story is **God dwelling with his people** in a renewed creation (Rev 21:3), the fulfillment of the Tabernacle and Temple. The hope is more the *presence of God* filling everything than our escape from "the presence of sin."

### 4.4 Side-by-side comparison

| Traditional formula | Relational / Jewish-lens reframing |
|---|---|
| Saved from the **penalty** of sin | Brought back into **covenant belonging**: forgiven, reconciled, adopted |
| Saved from the **power** of sin | **Walking in faithfulness** by the Spirit, Torah written on the heart |
| Saved from the **presence** of sin | **God's presence** filling a renewed creation, with resurrection life |
| Saved by His works, not ours | Saved by the **Messiah's faithfulness**, which we share through loyal trust |

## 5. How Defenders of the Traditional View Respond

- **It's a teaching summary.** The three-tense scheme simplifies; it doesn't claim each word appears in only one tense. Reformed theology already distinguishes *definitive* sanctification (1 Cor 6:11) from *progressive* sanctification.
- **Legal language is biblical.** Condemnation (Rom 8:1), curse (Gal 3:13), "counted as righteousness" (Rom 4:3–5) and substitution (Isa 53) are real biblical categories. Relational models don't remove them.
- **Imputation has textual grounds.** Defenders draw it from Rom 4–5, 2 Cor 5:21 and Phil 3:9, and see it as necessary to guard against salvation by performance.
- **Critics of the New Perspective** (D.A. Carson, Simon Gathercole, Stephen Westerholm) argue that Second Temple Judaism contained merit-based strands. They also argue that Paul's "works" includes moral obedience generally (Rom 4:4–5; Eph 2:8–9; Titus 3:5).
- **Both / and.** Many Protestants hold forensic and relational aspects together, especially through Calvin's emphasis on **union with Christ**.

Many readers end up treating the legal and relational pictures as complementary, with a Hebrew covenantal frame as the larger context.

## 6. Reflection Questions

1. Which picture of salvation shaped your faith most: courtroom, family, or covenant?
2. How does reading 1 Cor 6:11 and Rom 8:30 change your view of sanctification and glorification?
3. If faith (*emunah*) means loyalty and faithfulness, how does that change how you read James 2?
4. What difference does it make to hope for God's presence in a renewed creation rather than escape from the world?
