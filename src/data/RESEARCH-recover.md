# Recovery study page: verified data points

Key: VERIFIED = checked against a primary or authoritative source (abstract or official page read directly). PARTIAL = core fact confirmed but some detail only from secondary sources. DISPUTED = the figure is contested or commonly misquoted. NOT VERIFIED = could not confirm.
Note: Abstracts were read through the Europe PMC API (the record of each paper). PubMed and Cochrane pages blocked automated fetching, so the Europe PMC and DOI links are given instead.

## 1. Addiction relapse rates (NIDA)
- **Claim:** Relapse rates for substance use disorders are similar to those for other chronic illnesses.
- **Figures:** Drug addiction 40–60%; hypertension 50–70%; asthma 50–70%; type 1 diabetes 30–50%.
- **Source:** NIDA, *Drugs, Brains, and Behavior: The Science of Addiction*, "Treatment and Recovery" (page dated July 2020). The chart is taken from McLellan, Lewis, O'Brien & Kleber, "Drug dependence, a chronic medical illness", *JAMA* 2000;284:1689–1695.
- **URL:** https://nida.nih.gov/publications/drugs-brains-behavior-science-addiction/treatment-recovery
- **Confidence:** VERIFIED, with a caveat. The NIDA text says only that relapse rates are "similar". The percentages are in the chart image, cited to the JAMA paper. For hypertension and asthma, "relapse" in McLellan meant a return of symptoms, mostly through not taking medication. The comparison is fair, but the categories are not exactly the same. NIDA also says relapse "doesn't mean treatment has failed" and that "detoxification alone ... generally leads to resumption of drug use."

## 2. Cochrane 2020: Alcoholics Anonymous and Twelve-Step Facilitation
- **Claim:** Manualized AA/TSF beats other established treatments, such as CBT, for continuous abstinence.
- **Figures:** 27 studies, 10,565 participants. For continuous abstinence at 12 months, RR 1.21 (95% CI 1.03–1.42; 2 RCTs, n = 1,936; **high-certainty**), and the effect held at 24 and 36 months. The effect is often reported as about 42% abstinent versus about 35% (secondary sources) and described as "about 20% better". For percent days abstinent at 12 months, AA/TSF performed as well as other treatments (MD 3.03, very low certainty) and better at 24 months (MD 12.91) and 36 months (MD 6.64), with low certainty. On drinking intensity and consequences, AA/TSF performed as well as other treatments. Health-care costs: 3 of 4 economic studies found greater savings with AA/TSF, and AA/TSF "probably produces substantial healthcare cost savings."
- **Source:** Kelly JF, Humphreys K, Ferri M. "Alcoholics Anonymous and other 12-step programs for alcohol use disorder." *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2020, Issue 3, CD012880.pub2 (published 11 March 2020).
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012880.pub2 ; free full text: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC7065341
- **Confidence:** VERIFIED from the abstract. Caveats: 11 of 27 studies had high selection bias. The trials tested clinician-delivered TSF that encourages AA attendance, not AA itself, a point made by Saitz in a BU commentary. None of the trials included medication for AUD.

## 3. Smoking cessation
**3a. Unaided ("cold turkey") quitting**
- **Figure:** 3–5% of self-quitters achieve prolonged abstinence at 6–12 months after a given quit attempt. Most relapse happens in the first 8 days.
- **Source:** Hughes JR, Keely J, Naud S. "Shape of the relapse curve and long-term abstinence among untreated smokers." *Addiction* 2004;99(1):29–38.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2004.00540.x
- **Confidence:** VERIFIED from the abstract. Note: some argue that many quit attempts are half-hearted, which pulls the unaided figure down. Wikipedia gives a broader 4–7%.

**3b. CDC: quit attempts and success (US, 2018)**
- **Figures:** 55.1% of adult smokers made a past-year quit attempt (up from 52.8% in 2009). 7.5% achieved "recent successful cessation", meaning they had quit for at least 6 months (up from 6.3%). The quit ratio, the share of ever-smokers who have quit, was 61.7%. Adult cigarette smoking was 13.7% (34.2 million people). A companion CDC note says "nearly 70% want to quit" and "using counseling and medications increases the chances of quitting."
- **Source:** Creamer MR et al. "Tobacco Product Use and Cessation Indicators Among Adults — United States, 2018." *MMWR* 2019;68(45):1013–1019.
- **URL:** https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/68/wr/mm6845a2.htm (and https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/68/wr/mm6845a1.htm)
- **Confidence:** VERIFIED from the full text. "7.5% succeed" is a population figure that mixes assisted and unaided quitters.

**3c. Medication (Cochrane)**
- **Varenicline vs placebo:** RR 2.32 (95% CI 2.15–2.51; 41 RCTs, 17,395 people; high certainty). Varenicline beats single-form NRT (RR 1.25) and bupropion (RR 1.36). There is **no clear difference from combination NRT** (RR 1.02, 0.87–1.20). Cytisine vs placebo: RR 1.30. Source: Livingstone-Banks J et al., *Cochrane* 2023, CD006103.pub8. URL: https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006103.pub8 ; https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC10169257 . VERIFIED.
- **NRT vs control:** RR 1.55 (1.49–1.61; 133 trials, 64,640 people; high quality). NRT raises quit rates by 50–60% regardless of how much extra support is given. Source: Hartmann-Boyce J et al., *Cochrane* 2018, CD000146.pub5. URL: https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000146.pub5 ; https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC6353172 . VERIFIED.
- **How to frame it:** Medication roughly doubles quit rates (NRT about ×1.5, varenicline about ×2.3). Even so, most quit attempts fail. Applied to the 3–5% unaided baseline, that gives roughly 5–12%. Trial quit rates are higher because trial participants are motivated and supported. The ranges in this bullet are approximate inference, not a quoted figure.

## 4. Habit formation (Lally et al.)
- **Figures:** 96 volunteers (UCL) repeated a daily eating, drinking or exercise behaviour, cued by something in their routine, for 84 days. The median time to reach 95% of each person's automaticity plateau was **66 days**, with a range of **18–254 days**. Missing one day did not derail habit formation. Exercise habits took about 1.5× longer.
- **Source:** Lally P, van Jaarsveld CHM, Potts HWW, Wardle J. "How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world." *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40(6):998–1009. Published online July 2009; the issue is dated **2010**.
- **URLs:** https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674 ; UCL news: https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit ; BPS digest: https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit
- **Confidence:** PARTIAL. The figures are consistent across UCL and the BPS but were not read in the paper itself. Caveats: the 66 days applies only to the participants whose data fitted the model well (about 39 of the 96 by one account). **254 days is an extrapolation** beyond the 84-day study. Automaticity was self-reported. Cite the paper as "2009 (online) / 2010 (print)".

## 5. Psychotherapy effectiveness and PTSD guidelines
- **CBT overview:** Hofmann, Asnaani, Vonk, Sawyer & Fang, "The efficacy of cognitive behavioral therapy: a review of meta-analyses", *Cognitive Therapy and Research* 2012;36(5):427–440. The review covered 106 meta-analyses. Support was strongest for anxiety disorders, somatoform disorders, bulimia, anger control and general stress. URL: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC3584580 . PARTIAL: no single effect size was confirmed from it.
- **Depression psychotherapy effect size:** Cuijpers et al. 2010, *British Journal of Psychiatry*: 117 trials. Mean g = **0.67** against control, falling to **0.42** after adjusting for publication bias. Against pill placebo, g = 0.25 (Cuijpers 2013/14, *Psychological Medicine*). URL: https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/efficacy-of-cognitive-behavioural-therapy-and-other-psychological/ . PARTIAL (secondary abstract). Takeaway: therapy works, with a moderate effect that published trials overstate.
- **WHO (2013):** For adults with PTSD, the WHO recommends **trauma-focused CBT, EMDR and stress management**. It does not recommend psychological debriefing. Benzodiazepines should not be offered for acute stress symptoms. Source: *WHO guidelines on conditions specifically related to stress* (17 March 2013; ISBN 9789241505406). URL: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241505406 ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK159725/ . VERIFIED (WHO page plus secondary summaries). A 2023 WHO evidence profile says the 2013 stress recommendations were "not updated"; whether a 2023 mhGAP revision changed them is NOT VERIFIED.
- **APA (2017):** The *Clinical Practice Guideline for the Treatment of PTSD in Adults* (adopted 24 Feb 2017) **strongly** recommends CBT, cognitive processing therapy (CPT), cognitive therapy and prolonged exposure. It **conditionally** recommends brief eclectic psychotherapy, **EMDR** and narrative exposure therapy, and conditionally recommends the medications fluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline and venlafaxine. URL: https://www.apa.org/ptsd-guideline ; summary: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31305099/ . VERIFIED (secondary summaries agree). Correction to the brief: under the APA guideline, EMDR is *conditional*, not strong, while the WHO recommends it fully. "TF-CBT" is strictly the child protocol (Cohen, Mannarino & Deblinger); for adults, "trauma-focused CBT" is the umbrella term.

## 6. Religion, spirituality and addiction; faith-based programmes
- **Meta-analysis:** Yeung JW, Chan YC, Lee BL. "Youth religiosity and substance use: a meta-analysis from 1995 to 2007." *Psychological Reports* 2009;105(1):255–266. 22 studies. Mean weighted correlation **Zr = .16**, a protective effect that is significant across all definitions of religiosity and consistent for alcohol, cigarettes, marijuana and other illicit drugs. URL: https://doi.org/10.2466/PR0.105.1.255-266 . VERIFIED (abstract). The effect is small but consistent.
- **Prospective cohort:** Chen Y, VanderWeele TJ. *American Journal of Epidemiology* 2018;187(11):2355–2364. About 5,700–7,500 adolescents followed for 8–14 years. Attending religious services at least weekly was associated with **lower marijuana use**, later sexual initiation and fewer sexual partners, along with greater life satisfaction. Private prayer or meditation gave similar results. URL: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC6211237 . VERIFIED.
- **Broad review:** Koenig HG. "Religion, spirituality, and health: the research and clinical implications." *ISRN Psychiatry* 2012:278730. A systematic review of studies from 1872 to 2010 that includes substance abuse. URL: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC3671693 . The often-quoted statistic of "86% of studies show less substance use" was NOT VERIFIED; the "278" sometimes quoted alongside it is the article number.
- **Teen Challenge "86% success":** DISPUTED / NOT VERIFIED. The figure has been attributed to an HHS/NIDA study by Catherine Hess (1970s) and to a 1999 Northwestern University study, which appears to be Aaron Bicknese's PhD dissertation, *The Teen Challenge drug treatment program in comparative perspective* (ProQuest 9932136). That study looked at **graduates only** from three large centres, three years out. Weaknesses: the denominator counts graduates rather than everyone who entered, so dropouts are excluded; participants selected themselves; outcomes were self-reported; there was no randomized control; and the work is an unpublished dissertation rather than peer-reviewed. Other figures: a 1994 University of Tennessee study of Chattanooga graduates found 67% drug-free, and a Wilder Research survey (Minnesota graduates 2007–09) found 74% reporting no use in the previous 6 months. The only controlled comparison, Johnston 1973, had n = 17 per group. There are also serious abuse and neglect lawsuits and investigative reports, including the New Yorker in 2021 and Indiana in 2024–25; these are allegations, not findings. URLs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Teen_Challenge ; https://wng.org/articles/a-seed-in-good-soil-1617335557 ; https://www.proquest.com/docview/304514823
- **Celebrate Recovery:** Founded in **1991** at Saddleback Church (Lake Forest, CA) by John Baker, a recovering alcoholic who had attended AA, with Rick Warren's backing after Baker sent him a 13-page letter. It is a "Christ-centered" 12-step adaptation using 8 principles based on the Beatitudes. Christianity Today reports it is now in about 35,000 churches. Baker died in February 2021, aged 72. URL: https://www.christianitytoday.com/news/2021/february/died-celebrate-recovery-founder-john-baker-saddleback-churc.html . VERIFIED. No rigorous outcome trials of Celebrate Recovery were found.

## 7. Forgiveness interventions
- **Meta-analysis:** Wade NG, Hoyt WT, Kidwell JEM, Worthington EL Jr. "Efficacy of psychotherapeutic interventions to promote forgiveness: a meta-analysis." *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* 2014;82(1):154–170. 54 reports. 53 post-treatment effect sizes (N = 2,323). Compared with no treatment, Δ+ = **0.56** [0.43, 0.68]; compared with alternative treatments, Δ+ = **0.45** [0.21, 0.69]. Forgiveness treatments also produced greater reductions in **depression and anxiety** and gains in **hope** than no treatment. Longer treatment (dosage) and individual rather than group format predicted bigger effects. After controlling for moderators there was no difference between models. The advantage of individual over group format was clearest for Enright-model interventions. URL: https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035268 . VERIFIED (abstract).
- **Enright's process model:** 20 units in 4 phases. **Uncovering** (units 1–8) means facing the hurt and anger. **Decision** (9–11) means committing to forgive. **Work** (12–15) means reframing, empathy and compassion toward the offender, and accepting the pain, offered as a "moral gift". **Deepening** (16–20) means finding meaning and release. Popular book: *Forgiveness Is a Choice* (APA, 2001). URLs: https://internationalforgiveness.com/how-to-forgive/ ; https://education.wisc.edu/?p=3770 . VERIFIED. The model began as 17 units and was expanded to 20.

## 8. Compulsive sexual behaviour disorder (ICD-11) and moral incongruence
- **Classification:** ICD-11 code **6C72 Compulsive sexual behaviour disorder** is classed under **impulse control disorders**, *not* under "disorders due to substance use or addictive behaviours". It is defined as a persistent failure to control intense, repetitive sexual impulses, lasting ≥6 months and causing marked distress or impairment. Crucially, the definition states: "**Distress that is entirely related to moral judgments and disapproval about sexual impulses, urges, or behaviours is not sufficient to meet this requirement.**" ICD-11 was adopted by the World Health Assembly in May 2019 and took effect on 1 January 2022. So "**porn addiction**" or "sex addiction" is **not** a formal diagnosis in ICD-11 or DSM-5-TR. Source: Kraus SW et al. (incl. G. Reed of WHO). "Compulsive sexual behaviour disorder in the ICD-11." *World Psychiatry* 2018;17(1):109–110. URLs: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC5775124 ; ICD-11 browser: https://icd.who.int/browse/2024-01/mms/en#1630268048 . VERIFIED.
- **Moral incongruence:** Grubbs JB, Perry SL, Wilt JA, Reid RC. "Pornography problems due to moral incongruence: an integrative model with a systematic review and meta-analysis." *Archives of Sexual Behavior* 2019;48(2):397–415. Feeling "addicted" to pornography may in many cases be better explained by moral incongruence (behaviour conflicting with one's beliefs) than by dysregulated use. In this literature, religiosity predicts *perceived* addiction while being unrelated to, or inversely related to, actual frequency of use. URL: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-018-1248-x . PARTIAL: the model and conclusion were verified from the abstract; the pooled effect sizes were not read. The paper drew 8 published commentaries, so it is debated.
- **Pastoral point:** This research does **not** say that moral conviction is wrong. It says that guilt plus secrecy and shame can make use *feel* compulsive, and that clinicians should separate moral distress from loss of control.

## 9. Origins of secular approaches
- **AA:** AA's founding date is **10 June 1935** in Akron, Ohio: Bill Wilson's (a New York stockbroker) first meeting with Dr Bob Smith (an Akron surgeon) was 12 May 1935, and 10 June 1935 is Dr Bob's last drink. Both had been involved in the **Oxford Group**, an evangelical Christian movement led by Lutheran minister **Frank Buchman**. Rev. **Sam Shoemaker**, an Episcopal rector at Calvary Church in New York, led the American Oxford Group. Wilson wrote: early AA "got its ideas of self-examination, acknowledgment of character defects, restitution for harm done, and working with others straight from the Oxford Group and directly from Sam Shoemaker... and from nowhere else" (*A.A. Comes of Age*, 1957, p. 39; said at the 1955 convention). Shoemaker was a mentor, **not** a co-founder. AA broke from the Oxford Group around 1937–39. Its concept of powerlessness differs from the Oxford Group's "victory over sin" (Mercadante). URLs: https://www.aa.org/the-start-and-growth-of-aa ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Alcoholics_Anonymous ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Shoemaker . VERIFIED.
- **MBSR:** Founded **1979** by Jon Kabat-Zinn as the Stress Reduction Clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical School/Center. It is an 8-week secular course. Its acknowledged roots are **Zen** (Kabat-Zinn studied with Philip Kapleau, Thich Nhat Hanh and Seung Sahn), **Vipassana** (Theravada), yoga, and some Advaita Vedanta. Book: *Full Catastrophe Living* (1990). URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Kabat-Zinn . VERIFIED (encyclopedic).
- **REBT (Albert Ellis):** Ellis began rational therapy in **1955**. It was renamed rational emotive therapy (sources give 1959 or 1961) and REBT in **1992**. Ellis explicitly credited the Stoics, especially **Epictetus**, *Enchiridion* 5: "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of them" (Higginson/older translation; modern versions read "It is not things that disturb us, but our judgements about things"). He also credited Alfred Adler. URL: https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/health-and-medicine/rational-emotive-therapy . VERIFIED; the dates have minor source variance.
- **Cognitive therapy (Aaron Beck):** Developed in the **early 1960s** at the University of Pennsylvania. Beck's experiments set out to test the psychoanalytic theory of depression and failed to support it, leading him to the concept of "automatic thoughts". He won the Lasker Award in 2006 and died in 2021, aged 100. URLs: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC9768518 ; https://laskerfoundation.org/winners/development-of-cognitive-therapy-theory-and-practice/ . VERIFIED.
- **Logotherapy (Viktor Frankl):** The "third Viennese school", after Freud and Adler, built on the "will to meaning". Book: *Ein Psychologe erlebt das Konzentrationslager* (Vienna, **1946**). The English translation appeared as *From Death-Camp to Existentialism* (Beacon, 1959) and was retitled *Man's Search for Meaning* (about 1962). Frankl was Jewish. His work draws openly on transcendence and freedom of will, and he wrote of the "unconscious God" (*Der unbewusste Gott*, 1948). URLs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man%27s_Search_for_Meaning ; https://www.britannica.com/topic/logotherapy . VERIFIED.
- **Attachment theory (John Bowlby):** Key works are the WHO monograph *Maternal Care and Mental Health* (**1951**), "The nature of the child's tie to his mother" (*International Journal of Psycho-Analysis*, **1958**), and the *Attachment and Loss* trilogy (**1969, 1973, 1980**). URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maternal_deprivation ; https://www.srcd.org/sites/default/files/file-attachments/bowlby_cv.pdf . VERIFIED.
- **Self-compassion (Kristin Neff):** Neff KD (**2003**). "Self-compassion: An alternative conceptualization of a healthy attitude toward oneself." *Self and Identity* 2:85–101. A companion 2003 paper in the same journal introduced the Self-Compassion Scale. Neff explicitly drew on **Buddhist** psychology; she has practised Buddhist meditation since 1997. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristin_Neff ; https://self-compassion.org/the-research/ . VERIFIED.
- **Positive psychology (Martin Seligman):** Launched during Seligman's term as APA president in **1998**, through his presidential address and his APA Monitor column "Building human strength: psychology's forgotten mission" (1998). The address was published as "The President's Address", *American Psychologist* 1999;54:559–562. The landmark special issue is Seligman & Csikszentmihalyi, "Positive psychology: an introduction", *American Psychologist* **January 2000**. URL: https://ppc.sas.upenn.edu/node/48 . PARTIAL: the 1998 launch is verified; the 1999 citation is from memory.
- **Acknowledged religious influences, summary:** AA's is openly **Christian** (the Oxford Group and Shoemaker). MBSR and self-compassion openly draw on **Buddhism**. REBT and CBT draw on **Stoic** philosophy, and Ellis was himself an atheist. Frankl wrote openly on religion. For Bowlby, Beck and Seligman, no religious source was claimed in what I checked.

## 10. Christian approaches to psychology
- **Jay Adams:** *Competent to Counsel* (Presbyterian & Reformed, **1970**) launched "nouthetic" counselling. The name comes from the Greek *noutheteō*, to admonish. Adams co-founded **CCEF** (Christian Counseling & Educational Foundation) with John Bettler in **1968** near Philadelphia. He taught at Westminster Theological Seminary. NANC, founded 1976, became ACBC. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jay_E._Adams . VERIFIED (encyclopedic). Note: later CCEF figures such as Powlison and Welch softened Adams's approach.
- **"Five Views" book:** *Psychology & Christianity: Five Views*, ed. **Eric L. Johnson** (IVP Academic, 2nd ed. **2010**; the IVP page lists 20 Sept 2009). The 1st edition (2000) was edited by **Eric L. Johnson & Stanton L. Jones** and had **four** views (*Psychology & Christianity: Four Views*). The 2nd edition's views: levels-of-explanation (David Myers), integration (Stanton Jones), Christian psychology (Robert Roberts & P. J. Watson), transformational psychology (John Coe & Todd Hall), and biblical counseling (David Powlison). URL: https://www.ivpress.com/psychology-christianity . VERIFIED (2nd edition). The first-edition details are from memory, so check them before quoting "Five Views (Johnson & Jones)".
- **"All truth is God's truth":** The phrase itself is **not** a direct Augustine quote; it is a paraphrase. Augustine, *De doctrina Christiana* II.18 (§28 in some editions), Shaw translation: "**let every good and true Christian understand that wherever truth may be found, it belongs to his Master**." Green's 1995 translation renders it as truth belonging to the Christian's "Lord". The surrounding context is the "spoiling the Egyptians" argument for using pagan learning. URL: https://logoslibrary.org/augustine/doctrine/218.html ; https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/12022.htm . VERIFIED. **Arthur F. Holmes**, *All Truth Is God's Truth* (Eerdmans, **1977**, 145 pp.). Holmes taught philosophy at Wheaton College from 1951 to 1994. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_F._Holmes . VERIFIED.

## 11. Concerns about inner healing and therapy
- **Theophostic Prayer Ministry:** Developed in the mid-1990s by Ed Smith, a Baptist minister in Campbellsville, KY, and later renamed **Transformation Prayer Ministry** (TPM). Its theory: lies, some attributed to demonic influence, are embedded in traumatic memories, and Jesus reveals truth in those memories during prayer. Criticisms: (a) **false or recovered memory** risk, including alleged false abuse accusations (*Cultic Studies Review* 2003); (b) no controlled outcome studies, only case reports and surveys; (c) theological objections (Bryan Maier, *Trinity Journal* 24:2, 2003; Bobgans). The Christian Research Institute judged later versions to be orthodox but noted that the efficacy claims are unproven. Smith revised the teaching and the name over time. URLs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theophostic_Prayer_Ministry ; https://www.equip.org/articles/teachings-in-transition/ . VERIFIED (secondary). The exact year of the rename was not confirmed (about 2012–2014).
- **False memory research:** Loftus EF & Pickrell JE (1995). "The formation of false memories." *Psychiatric Annals* 25:720–725. In the "lost in the mall" study, **about 25% of 24 participants** came to remember, partly or fully, a fabricated childhood event. A preregistered replication (Murphy et al., *Memory* 2023;31:818–830; n = 123) found **35%**. URL: https://cora.ucc.ie/handle/10468/14378 . VERIFIED. The original study is small and its definition of "memory" has been criticised, but the replication strengthens the finding.
- **"The Therapeutic Gospel":** David Powlison, *Journal of Biblical Counseling* **25:3** (2007). The essay argues that the therapeutic gospel serves "felt needs" (love, significance, self-esteem, pleasure) and "discards the glory of God in Christ". It argues that real change comes "through repentance, faith, and transformation into the image of the Son." URL: https://www.ccef.org/jbc-article/the-therapeutic-gospel . VERIFIED: volume and issue are from CCEF, and vol. 25 = 2007. The essay was also circulated earlier through 9Marks (not verified).

## 12. The brain, habit and shame
- **Habit loop:** Charles Duhigg, *The Power of Habit* (Random House, **2012**), popularised the habit loop of **cue → routine → reward**, drawing on Ann **Graybiel**'s MIT lab. Her rat-maze studies showed that striatal (basal ganglia) neurons fire throughout a task at first. Once the habit forms, firing "brackets" or "chunks" the routine: activity concentrates at the start and end of the task. Later work is Martiros, Burgess & Graybiel, *Current Biology* 2018. URL: https://news.mit.edu/2018/distinctive-brain-pattern-helps-habits-form-0208 . PARTIAL: Graybiel's work is verified; that Duhigg cites her is well known but was not checked in the book. "Cue-routine-reward" is Duhigg's simplification, not Graybiel's terminology. Dopamine signals reward *prediction*, and a cue alone comes to trigger craving (Schultz).
- **Shame and relapse:** Randles D & Tracy JL. "Nonverbal displays of shame predict relapse and declining health in recovering alcoholics." *Clinical Psychological Science* **2013**;1(2):149–155. N = 105 newly sober AA members (sober less than 6.5 months), followed up about 4 months later. **Nonverbal shame displays** (shoulders slumped, chest narrowed) while describing their last drink predicted relapse, relapse severity and worsening health. **Self-reported shame did not predict outcomes, and neither did self-reported guilt.** URLs: https://doi.org/10.1177/2167702612470645 ; https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/shame-about-past-alcoholism-predicts-relapse-and-declining-health-in-recovering-alcoholics.html . VERIFIED. **Correction:** this study does *not* show that "guilt predicts repair"; guilt simply had no effect in it.
- **Better source for "guilt predicts repair":** Tangney JP, Stuewig J, Martinez AG. "Two faces of shame: the roles of shame and guilt in predicting recidivism." *Psychological Science* **2014**;25(3):799–805. 476 jail inmates. **Guilt-proneness directly predicted *lower* reoffending** in the first year after release. Shame-proneness predicted *more* reoffending, but only through blaming others (externalisation). URL: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC4105017 . VERIFIED. Wider literature (Tangney): guilt focuses on "I did a bad thing" and motivates repair; shame focuses on "I am bad" and motivates hiding or blaming.

## 13. Social connection and addiction
- **Rat Park:** Bruce K. Alexander and colleagues, Simon Fraser University. The studies were run in the late 1970s and published **1978–1981** (*Psychopharmacology* 1978; *Pharmacology Biochemistry & Behavior* 1981). Rats in a large, enriched "park" with other rats drank less morphine solution than isolated caged rats. **Replication problems:** Petrie BF (1996). "Environment is not the most important variable in determining oral morphine consumption in Wistar rats." *Psychological Reports* 78(2):391–400. The study failed to replicate the effect; strain differences were suggested as the reason. The originals had small samples and methodological issues, and *Science* and *Nature* declined them. Later rodent work on social and environmental enrichment (for example, Venniro et al. 2018, *Nature Neuroscience*: rats chose social interaction over heroin or meth) supports the general idea more than Rat Park itself. That 2018 citation is from memory and was not verified. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rat_Park ; https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/9728/ . VERIFIED for Rat Park and Petrie. The popular "Rat Park proves addiction is only about connection" version is **overstated**.
- **Social relationships and mortality:** Holt-Lunstad J, Smith TB, Layton JB. "Social relationships and mortality risk: a meta-analytic review." *PLoS Medicine* **2010**;7(7):e1000316. 148 studies, 308,849 participants. **OR = 1.50** (1.42–1.59), a **50% greater likelihood of survival** with stronger social relationships. The effect was largest for complex measures of social integration (OR 1.91). The authors call it "comparable with well-established risk factors" such as smoking and alcohol. URL: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316 ; https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC2910600 . VERIFIED. **Caution:** the popular line "loneliness is as deadly as smoking 15 cigarettes a day" is a loose paraphrase, repeated in the 2023 US Surgeon General's advisory as "*up to* 15 cigarettes". It is not a finding of the paper, and the paper measured social relationships broadly, not loneliness. A 2015 follow-up meta-analysis reported increased mortality risk of 26% for loneliness, 29% for social isolation and 32% for living alone.

## 14. Alcohol withdrawal danger
- **Claim:** Alcohol withdrawal can kill, which is why medical detox matters. Alcohol, along with benzodiazepines and barbiturates, is one of the few drugs whose withdrawal is potentially fatal.
- **Figures:** About 3–5% of people withdrawing from alcohol develop severe withdrawal or delirium tremens (DTs). DTs usually start **48–96 hours** after the last drink (StatPearls says as early as 48 h) and last up to 5 days. **Mortality is up to 37% without appropriate treatment.** With modern treatment the figure is commonly put at about 1–5%; sources vary, and some say less than 5% or 5–15%. Withdrawal seizures usually occur 6–48 hours after the last drink.
- **Source:** Rahman A, Paul M. "Delirium Tremens." *StatPearls* (NCBI Bookshelf, updated regularly).
- **URL:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482134/
- **Confidence:** VERIFIED for the 37% untreated figure and the 3–5% incidence figure. The treated-mortality figure varies by source (DISPUTED range). NIDA likewise warns that relapse after abstinence can lead to fatal overdose because tolerance falls (see item 1).
